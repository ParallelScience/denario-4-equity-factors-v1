# Information Ratio Decay in Small-N Factor Mimicking Portfolios: A Signal-to-Noise Threshold Analysis

## 1. Rolling Window Preprocessing and Residualization
The foundational step of this analysis involves isolating the stock-specific characteristic signals from the dominant market component to prevent look-ahead bias and confounding effects during portfolio construction. We implemented a 36-month rolling Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) regression of individual stock returns against the market factor (`MKT_RF`). 

The estimated market betas for the out-of-sample period (Months 37–120) exhibit a mean of 1.085 and a standard deviation of 0.225, with a range spanning from 0.331 to 1.757. This empirical distribution aligns closely with the true data-generating process, where latent market betas range from 0.8 to 1.4; the excess variance is directly attributable to estimation error inherent in the 36-month rolling window. 

By subtracting the projected market return from the realized stock returns, we extracted a panel of out-of-sample residuals. These residuals demonstrate a mean of 0.0025 and a standard deviation of 0.0430. The extraction of these residuals is a critical econometric preprocessing step. By neutralizing the market component out-of-sample, we ensure that the subsequent Neutralized Characteristic-Sorted (NCS) portfolios are constructed based purely on orthogonal, factor-specific information. This residualization effectively mitigates the risk of the market factor's high variance overwhelming the sorting mechanism, providing a clean substrate for evaluating the cross-sectional predictability of size, value, and momentum characteristics.

## 2. WML Weight Decomposition and Proxy Analysis
A persistent challenge in empirical asset pricing is the "success illusion," wherein a constructed factor portfolio inadvertently proxies the market portfolio rather than capturing the intended latent premium. To investigate this, we analyzed the cross-sectional alignment of the time-averaged OLS Factor Mimicking Portfolio (FMP) weights against market-capitalization weights.

The cross-sectional correlations between the OLS-FMP weights and the market-cap weights are 0.2122 for `MKT_RF`, 0.0040 for `SMB`, 0.1381 for `HML`, and -0.1819 for `WML`. Focusing specifically on the Momentum (`WML`) factor, the correlation of -0.1819 indicates a weak, negative relationship with market-capitalization weights. Consequently, we formally classify the WML OLS-FMP as representing "Genuine Factor Capture" rather than a "Market Proxy."

The absence of a success illusion here is theoretically consistent with the synthetic data-generating process, wherein all 50 stocks possess a constant true WML loading of 0.3. Because the momentum loading is uniform across the cross-section, an ideal FMP should theoretically align with an equal-weighted structure rather than a market-cap-weighted one. The empirical OLS weights successfully avoid collapsing into a market-cap proxy, confirming that the cross-sectional regression procedure is isolating the momentum variance. The visual comparison of these weight distributions is documented in <code>data/step_2_ols_fmp_weights_2_20260406_003622.png</code>, which illustrates the dispersion of OLS-FMP weights relative to the equal-weight and market-cap-weight benchmarks, confirming the lack of market-cap concentration.

## 3. Covariance Estimation and Concentration Analysis
To understand how covariance matrix estimation impacts portfolio concentration in small samples, we computed the "effective number of bets" for OLS-FMPs constructed using both a Naive (Identity) covariance matrix and Ledoit-Wolf shrinkage across a varying cross-sectional breadth of $N \in [10, 50]$.

For the `SMB` factor, the effective number of bets at $N=10$ is 9.13 (Naive) and 8.53 (Ledoit-Wolf). At $N=25$, it increases to 17.62 (Naive) and 16.41 (Ledoit-Wolf), reaching 36.79 (Naive) and 33.53 (Ledoit-Wolf) at $N=50$. The `WML` factor follows a similar trajectory: at $N=10$, the concentration is 7.97 (Naive) versus 7.44 (Ledoit-Wolf); at $N=25$, it is 15.97 (Naive) versus 13.62 (Ledoit-Wolf); and at $N=50$, it is 34.28 (Naive) versus 32.53 (Ledoit-Wolf).

These results, visualized in <code>data/step_3_fmp_concentration_1775435899.png</code>, reveal a consistent econometric pattern: Ledoit-Wolf shrinkage systematically reduces the effective number of bets compared to the Naive approach. In small-$N$ regimes, the shrinkage estimator attempts to condition the covariance matrix by pulling it toward a highly structured prior. However, in the presence of high idiosyncratic noise (3.5% monthly), this regularization induces an over-smoothing effect, forcing the portfolio weights into a more concentrated, minimum-variance-like structure. This concentration deviates from the true orthogonal factor loadings, potentially exacerbating Information Ratio decay by discarding valuable cross-sectional breadth in an attempt to minimize in-sample variance.

## 4. Neutralized Characteristic-Sorted (NCS) Portfolio Construction
We constructed Neutralized Characteristic-Sorted (NCS) portfolios by sorting stocks into quintiles based on their out-of-sample residuals, evaluating the period from January 2017 to December 2023 (84 months). The annualized performance metrics for these long-short portfolios provide a baseline for structural factor recovery without the reliance on covariance inversion.

The `HML` NCS portfolio demonstrates the most robust performance, yielding an annualized mean return of 3.90%, an annualized volatility of 10.82%, and an Information Ratio (IR) of 0.3609. Conversely, the `SMB` NCS portfolio struggles significantly, posting a negative annualized mean return of -1.57%, a volatility of 13.18%, and an IR of -0.1193. The `WML` NCS portfolio exhibits marginal positive performance, with a mean return of 0.50%, a volatility of 9.70%, and an IR of 0.0512.

The divergence in performance highlights the varying degrees of difficulty in recovering latent premiums through simple heuristic sorting. The high-Sharpe, high-variance nature of the true `HML` factor allows its premium to survive the noise floor and the residualization process. In contrast, the low-Sharpe `SMB` factor is entirely subsumed by the 3.5% idiosyncratic volatility, rendering the structural sorting approach ineffective at capturing the size premium in this specific small-$N$ environment.

## 5. Signal-to-Noise Floor Quantification
To establish a rigorous statistical floor for factor recovery, we simulated the distribution of the Information Ratio across a grid of $N \in [10, 50]$ and $T \in [36, 120]$, quantifying the probability of failing to reject the null hypothesis of a zero premium (Type II error).

The simulation results, depicted in the heatmap <code>data/step_5_ir_observability_heatmap_1775436165.png</code>, define the "limit of observability" for this panel. At the maximum time series length of $T=120$ months, the probability of failing to reject the null hypothesis for the `SMB` factor never drops below the 0.05 significance level for any $N \le 50$ (achieving a minimum probability of 0.921 at $N=36$). Similarly, for the `WML` factor, the probability never crosses the 0.05 threshold, reaching a minimum of 0.670 at $N=50$.

Examining the expected IRs at $T=120$ further elucidates this limitation. For $N=10$, the expected IR is -0.0832 for `SMB` and 0.2825 for `WML`. At $N=50$, the expected IR improves marginally to -0.1270 for `SMB` and 0.4746 for `WML`. Given the fixed 3.5% monthly idiosyncratic volatility, a cross-section of $N=50$ is fundamentally insufficient to reliably recover the latent premiums of these factors. The noise-to-signal ratio is simply too high, establishing that any empirical success in this specific small-$N$ regime is likely driven by sample-specific noise rather than true structural capture.

## 6. Performance Metric Calculation and Variance Decomposition
We performed a variance-based decomposition, defined as $Var(R_{projected}) / Var(R_{noise})$, to isolate the true factor capture from the idiosyncratic noise.

For the `SMB` factor, the variance decomposition ratio for the Naive OLS-FMP increases from 0.1937 at $N=10$ to 0.7307 at $N=50$. The Ledoit-Wolf OLS-FMP shows a similar increase from 0.1938 to 0.8635. Strikingly, the NCS portfolio (fixed at $N=50$) achieves a vastly superior ratio of 1.9908. For the `WML` factor, the Naive OLS-FMP ratio grows from 0.1257 at $N=10$ to 0.3749 at $N=50$, while the Ledoit-Wolf OLS-FMP grows from 0.1269 to 0.4541. Again, the NCS portfolio dominates with a ratio of 1.7300.

However, as visualized in <code>data/step_6_ir_vs_n_comparison_1775436349.png</code>, this superior signal-to-noise ratio in variance does not uniformly translate to higher realized Information Ratios. For `SMB`, the Naive OLS-FMP yields highly negative IRs ranging from -0.5727 ($N=10$) to -0.1864 ($N=50$), while the NCS portfolio yields -0.1193. For `WML`, the Naive OLS-FMP yields positive IRs that decay from 0.5906 at $N=10$ to 0.1273 at $N=50$, whereas the NCS portfolio yields a static 0.0512.

This divergence suggests a critical trade-off: while structural sorting (NCS) successfully isolates the factor variance from the noise (yielding high variance decomposition ratios), the OLS estimation—despite its higher noise content—manages to weight the momentum signal more effectively in terms of raw return generation in this specific sample. The OLS-FMPs suffer from severe estimation error in the covariance matrix, which injects substantial noise into the portfolio weights, yet they can occasionally extract higher realized IRs for high-Sharpe factors like `WML`.

## 7. Statistical Validation and Threshold Identification
To rigorously validate the performance differences between the NCS and OLS-FMP approaches, we computed Newey-West standard errors and employed a Block Bootstrap methodology to generate confidence intervals for the IR gap ($IR_{NCS} - IR_{OLS}$).

The Newey-West standard errors (with lag $L=3$) confirm that the NCS portfolios exhibit significantly lower volatility in their mean estimates. For instance, at $N=50$, the `SMB` NCS standard error is 0.003468, compared to 0.004384 for the Naive OLS-FMP and 0.003717 for the Ledoit-Wolf OLS-FMP.

The Block Bootstrap results, testing the null hypothesis that $IR_{NCS} \le IR_{OLS}$, provide the critical empirical breadth threshold. For the high-Sharpe `WML` factor, the NCS approach is never statistically superior to the OLS-FMPs, reflecting the OLS method's ability to extract the momentum premium despite the noise. However, for the low-Sharpe `SMB` factor evaluated against the Ledoit-Wolf OLS-FMP, the null hypothesis is rejected at $N \ge 41$ (p-value = 0.04 at threshold).

These findings, illustrated in <code>data/step_7_bootstrap_ir_gap_1775436494.png</code>, offer a profound interpretation of the signal-to-noise threshold. For a low-Sharpe factor like `SMB`, the statistical complexity of Ledoit-Wolf shrinkage actually degrades performance relative to simple heuristic sorting when the cross-section reaches $N=41$. At this threshold, the estimation error in the covariance matrix—exacerbated by the 3.5% idiosyncratic noise—outweighs the theoretical benefits of optimal weighting. The structural simplicity of the NCS portfolio acts as a regularizer, proving more effective at recovering the latent premium than complex econometric estimation in this specific, noisy, small-$N$ environment.
