### Results and Discussion

**1. Baseline Performance and the Ideal FMP Benchmark**

To establish a rigorous and objective benchmark for factor premium recovery, the out-of-sample performance of the "Ideal Factor Mimicking Portfolios" (FMPs) is first evaluated. These portfolios are constructed using the ground-truth factor loadings (`true_betas.csv`) and the full-sample residual covariance matrix. The construction relies on the standard projection matrix approach, defined as $w = \Sigma^{-1}\beta(\beta'\Sigma^{-1}\beta)^{-1}$. This methodology is designed to create a "pure play" portfolio that maintains a unit exposure to the target factor while strictly enforcing zero exposure to all other factors in the model. By utilizing the true parameters, the Ideal FMP isolates the theoretical maximum performance achievable given the specific sample realization and the inherent idiosyncratic noise of the data generating process.

Over the 84-month out-of-sample evaluation period (months 37 to 120), the Ideal FMPs yield annualized Information Ratios (IRs) of 0.352924 for the Market factor (MKT_RF), -0.139873 for the Size factor (SMB), 0.236521 for the Value factor (HML), and 0.266636 for the Momentum factor (WML). 

It is critical to contextualize these Ideal IRs against the true underlying Sharpe ratios of the data generating process (MKT_RF: 0.26, SMB: -0.21, HML: 0.59, WML: 0.81). The discrepancy between the true Sharpe ratios and the realized Ideal IRs highlights the profound impact of finite-sample realization. Even with perfect ex-ante knowledge of the factor structure and stock characteristics, the specific sequence of idiosyncratic shocks over the 84-month path heavily penalizes the realized performance. The persistent 3.5% monthly idiosyncratic volatility (approximately 12.1% annualized) injected into the panel establishes a highly challenging baseline. This structural noise floor dictates that any Sample-Estimated FMP must contend not only with estimation error but also with a fundamentally low signal-to-noise ratio.

**2. Information Ratio Decay and the Impact of Cross-Sectional Breadth**

The core of the empirical analysis examines how the performance of Sample-Estimated FMPs evolves as the cross-sectional breadth ($N$) is systematically varied from 10 to 50. To avoid look-ahead bias, these portfolios are constructed using a 36-month rolling window to estimate both the time-varying factor loadings ($\hat{\beta}$) and the residual covariance matrix ($\hat{\Sigma}$). For each $N$, 500 iterations of stratified random sampling are performed, strictly maintaining the original proportions of small/large cap and value/growth characteristics to ensure that the cross-sectional dispersion remains constant. The performance degradation is quantified using the IR Ratio, defined as the mean Sample-Estimated IR divided by the Ideal IR.

The empirical results reveal highly heterogeneous decay profiles across the four factors, underscoring the varying degrees of difficulty in recovering different types of risk premiums. For the Market factor (MKT_RF), the IR Ratio remains remarkably stable and close to parity, starting at 0.999792 at $N=10$ and ending at 0.979958 at $N=50$. Because the market beta ranges from 0.8 to 1.4 across all stocks, the market factor represents the dominant source of common variation. Consequently, it is easily recovered even with a minimal cross-section and exhibits high robustness to covariance estimation error.

Conversely, the Size factor (SMB) exhibits a pronounced degradation in premium capture. The IR Ratio for SMB is 0.798472 at $N=10$ and decays further to 0.719798 at $N=50$. The true SMB factor possesses a negative Sharpe ratio (-0.21) and a relatively small premium magnitude (-2.0% annualized). The sample estimation struggles to isolate this weak size effect from the overwhelming idiosyncratic noise. The sample FMP captures significantly less of the negative premium than the Ideal FMP, highlighting the severe difficulty of recovering low-signal, zero-investment factors in small-$N$ environments.

A highly counter-intuitive phenomenon is observed for the Value (HML) and Momentum (WML) factors. Rather than decaying, the IR Ratios for these factors increase significantly as $N$ expands, reaching 2.138117 for HML and 1.990909 for WML at $N=50$. This implies that the Sample-Estimated FMPs achieve out-of-sample Information Ratios roughly twice as high as the Ideal FMPs. This anomaly can be attributed to the dynamic nature of the rolling 36-month estimation window compared to the static, full-sample parameters used for the Ideal FMP. Because the synthetic data generating process features persistent stock characteristics (e.g., the momentum loading is a constant 0.3 for all stocks), the rolling estimation of $\hat{\beta}$ inadvertently captures short-term, spurious correlations between the idiosyncratic noise and the factor returns. When these spurious correlations persist over the out-of-sample holding period, the rolling FMP temporarily achieves higher Sharpe ratios. However, this represents an artifact of in-sample overfitting translating to the out-of-sample period, rather than a superior extraction of the true latent premium.

**3. The Curse of Dimensionality and Covariance Estimation Error**

A critical theoretical relationship in cross-sectional asset pricing is the trade-off between cross-sectional diversification and covariance estimation error. Theoretically, as the cross-sectional breadth $N$ increases, idiosyncratic noise should be diversified away at a rate proportional to $1/\sqrt{N}$, leading to a reduction in the standard error (SE) of the portfolio returns.

The empirical results perfectly illustrate this theoretical benefit up to a critical inflection point. For the high-Sharpe Momentum factor (WML), the annualized standard error of the mean premium decreases monotonically from 0.008032 at $N=10$ to a minimum of 0.004954 at $N=30$. This 38% reduction in SE confirms that expanding the cross-section successfully washes out a significant portion of the 3.5% monthly idiosyncratic volatility, exactly as portfolio theory predicts.

However, as $N$ approaches and exceeds the rolling estimation window length ($T=36$ months), the standard error begins to rise sharply, reaching 0.007216 at $N=50$. This non-monotonic behavior is a direct and severe manifestation of the curse of dimensionality. The sample covariance matrix $\hat{\Sigma}$ is estimated from the residuals of the 36-month rolling regression. With $K=4$ factors, there are only 31 degrees of freedom available to estimate the $N(N+1)/2$ unique elements of the covariance matrix. For $N=30$, there are 465 elements, making the matrix highly ill-conditioned. For $N \ge 40$, the matrix becomes strictly rank-deficient and singular.

To construct the FMP weights when $N > 36$, the methodology relies on the Moore-Penrose pseudo-inverse. While mathematically tractable, the pseudo-inverse resolves the singularity by performing a singular value decomposition and inverting only the non-zero singular values. This effectively projects the portfolio weights onto a lower-dimensional subspace spanned by the 36 months of returns, discarding orthogonal cross-sectional information. This structural break in the estimation process explains the degradation in the stability of the FMP returns. The estimation error in $\hat{\Sigma}^{-1}$ completely overwhelms the diversification benefits of adding more assets. Consequently, the mean annualized premium for WML drops from 0.015344 at $N=10$ to 0.005420 at $N=30$, before artificially rising to 0.011932 at $N=50$ due to the concentrated, unstable leverage induced by the pseudo-inverse. This demonstrates that in small-$T$ environments, simply increasing $N$ without regularization is highly counterproductive for out-of-sample stability.

**4. Statistical Thresholds and the Critical Breadth**

The primary objective of this study is to identify the "critical breadth"—defined as the minimum cross-sectional size $N$ required for the Sample-Estimated FMP to yield a statistically significant factor premium. Statistical significance is evaluated using Newey-West standard errors, with a lag length determined by the integer part of $4(T/100)^{2/9}$, to robustly account for potential autocorrelation and heteroskedasticity in the realized FMP returns.

Strikingly, the analysis reveals that the critical breadth is undefined (NaN) for all four factors across the entire tested range of $N \in [10, 50]$. None of the Sample-Estimated FMPs achieve a Newey-West t-statistic greater than the 1.96 threshold required for 95% confidence over the 84-month out-of-sample period.

This universal failure to cross the statistical threshold is a direct consequence of the severe noise-to-signal ratio. The idiosyncratic volatility of 3.5% per month is simply too large to be diversified away by a maximum of 50 stocks. The contrast between the low-Sharpe SMB factor and the high-Sharpe WML factor further illuminates the mechanics of this challenge.

The SMB factor, burdened by a weak underlying signal, is completely buried by the idiosyncratic noise. Its maximum absolute t-statistic is a mere -0.666776 (achieved at $N=30$), indicating that the recovered premium is statistically indistinguishable from zero regardless of the cross-sectional breadth employed.

In stark contrast, the WML factor, which possesses a strong true Sharpe ratio of 0.81, performs significantly better but still falls short. It achieves a maximum t-statistic of 1.910506 at $N=10$. The fact that WML achieves its highest t-statistic at the smallest cross-sectional breadth ($N=10$) is highly revealing. At $N=10$, the covariance matrix is well-conditioned ($10 \ll 36$), minimizing estimation error. The strong momentum signal is sufficient to generate a near-significant premium. However, as $N$ increases, the escalating covariance estimation error degrades the t-statistic faster than diversification can improve it. Similarly, the Market factor achieves its maximum t-statistic of 1.368851 at $N=15$, and the Value factor reaches 1.405842 at $N=50$, both failing to reject the null hypothesis of a zero premium.

**5. Synthesis and Implications for Factor Modeling**

The findings of this study provide a rigorous empirical boundary for cross-sectional asset pricing research and portfolio construction. This study conclusively demonstrates that a cross-sectional breadth of $N=50$ is fundamentally insufficient to reliably recover latent factor premiums using standard OLS regressions and sample covariance estimation when assets are subject to persistent idiosyncratic volatility of 3.5% per month.

The theoretical relationship between the noise-to-signal ratio and cross-sectional breadth dictates that researchers and practitioners face a strict dual mandate. First, to achieve statistical significance for standard equity factors, the cross-sectional universe must be significantly expanded ($N \gg 50$) to provide adequate diversification against high idiosyncratic noise. Second, and more critically, if the cross-section is expanded, the time-series estimation window must be proportionally increased ($T \gg N$) to maintain a well-conditioned covariance matrix.

In environments where $N$ is constrained—such as sector-specific studies, emerging markets, or specialized asset classes—or where rapid rolling windows are required to capture time-varying betas, traditional projection matrix approaches are statistically fragile. The observed degradation in standard errors and the failure to achieve critical breadth highlight the absolute necessity of employing noise-reduction techniques.

To successfully extract factor premiums in small-$N$, high-noise panels, researchers must abandon pure sample covariance estimation. Instead, they must turn to covariance regularization methods (such as Ledoit-Wolf shrinkage), utilize diagonalized covariance matrices that ignore off-diagonal correlations to preserve degrees of freedom, or rely on characteristic-based sorting and rank-weighted portfolios that implicitly bypass the need for covariance inversion entirely. Failure to account for the curse of dimensionality in these settings will inevitably lead to severe Type II errors, where true underlying risk premiums are dismissed as statistically insignificant due to overwhelming estimation noise.
