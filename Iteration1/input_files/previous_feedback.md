The current analysis successfully identifies the "curse of dimensionality" and the failure of the sample covariance matrix in small-$T$ environments. However, the interpretation of the "IR Ratio > 1" for HML and WML as purely "spurious overfitting" is a missed opportunity to address the fundamental flaw in the research design: the use of a **full-sample** Ideal FMP benchmark against a **rolling-window** Sample-Estimated FMP.

**1. Methodological Critique:**
*   **Benchmark Mismatch:** Comparing a rolling-window estimate (which adapts to local noise) against a full-sample Ideal FMP (which uses look-ahead information from the entire 120-month period) is a category error. The "outperformance" of the sample-estimated portfolios is likely not just overfitting, but a result of the Ideal FMP being "too static" to capture the specific realization of the synthetic noise in the out-of-sample period.
*   **The Pseudo-Inverse Trap:** You correctly identified that the Moore-Penrose pseudo-inverse is the source of instability for $N > 36$. However, continuing to use it for $N=40$ and $N=50$ is methodologically unsound. It is well-known that inverting a singular matrix produces extreme, non-economic weights. This is not a "result" of the data; it is a failure of the estimator.

**2. Actionable Improvements:**
*   **Replace the Covariance Estimator:** Instead of the pseudo-inverse, implement a **Ledoit-Wolf shrinkage** estimator or a **diagonal (constant correlation) covariance matrix**. This is the standard industry solution for the $N > T$ problem. It will allow you to test if the "critical breadth" is a function of the *estimator* or the *data*. If the threshold remains undefined with shrinkage, you have a much stronger claim about the data's signal-to-noise ratio.
*   **Refine the Ideal Benchmark:** Re-calculate the "Ideal FMP" using a rolling-window approach (using true betas but the same rolling covariance window as the sample-estimated version). This will isolate the impact of *loading estimation error* from *covariance estimation error*.
*   **Shift from IR to "Signal-to-Noise" Decomposition:** Rather than focusing on the IR (which is highly sensitive to the denominator's volatility), decompose the portfolio return into the "Factor Component" (True Beta * Factor Return) and the "Noise Component" (Residuals * Weights). This will visually demonstrate exactly how much idiosyncratic noise is being "picked up" by the weights as $N$ increases.

**3. Strategic Insight:**
*   **Stop the "Search for Significance":** You have already proven that with $N=50$ and $\sigma=3.5\%$, the signal is buried. Do not spend further iterations trying to find a "critical breadth" using OLS. The next iteration should focus on **robustness to noise**.
*   **Proposed Next Step:** Compare the performance of the OLS-based FMP against a **Characteristic-Sorted Portfolio** (e.g., quintile sorts). Characteristic sorts do not require covariance inversion and are immune to the $N > T$ singularity. If the characteristic-sorted portfolio outperforms the OLS-FMP, you have a powerful, actionable conclusion: *In small-N panels, structural simplicity (sorting) dominates statistical complexity (OLS/Markowitz).* This is a much more valuable scientific contribution than documenting the failure of an ill-conditioned matrix.
