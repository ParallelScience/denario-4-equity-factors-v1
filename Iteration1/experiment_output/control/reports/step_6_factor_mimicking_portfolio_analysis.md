### Results

#### 1. Ideal FMP Benchmark
The analysis commences with the evaluation of the Ideal Factor Mimicking Portfolio (FMP), constructed utilizing the ground-truth factor loadings. The primary theoretical objective of this benchmark is to isolate the impact of covariance matrix estimation error from the errors-in-variables problem inherent in sample-estimated cross-sectional loadings. By applying the Ledoit-Wolf constant-correlation shrinkage estimator to the rolling 36-month covariance matrices (with the average shrinkage delta ranging from 0.9888 at $N=10$ to 0.8020 at $N=50$), the Ideal FMP provides a theoretical upper bound on factor recovery given the finite cross-sectional breadth ($N$).

The empirical results reveal a stark divergence in the spanning capabilities of the Ideal FMP across different factors. For factors characterized by significant cross-sectional variation in their true loadings, the Ideal FMP demonstrates a robust, monotonic improvement in signal fidelity as $N$ increases. Specifically, the correlation between the Ideal FMP returns and the realized target factor returns for the Size factor (SMB) surges from a negligible 0.036 at $N=10$ to a highly accurate 0.942 at $N=50$. Similarly, the Value factor (HML) correlation improves from 0.796 to 0.901 over the same domain. Consequently, the annualized Information Ratios (IR) for these factors align closely with their true underlying Sharpe ratios as the cross-section expands. At $N=50$, the Ideal FMP yields an IR of -0.132 for SMB (true Sharpe: -0.21) and 0.543 for HML (true Sharpe: 0.59).

Conversely, the Ideal FMP exhibits anomalous behavior when attempting to recover the Momentum factor (WML). Despite the true WML factor possessing the highest annualized Sharpe ratio (0.81), the Ideal FMP IR degrades from 0.045 at $N=10$ to -0.488 at $N=50$. This degradation is a direct mathematical consequence of the underlying data generating process, wherein all equities are assigned a uniform, constant WML loading of 0.3. In the absence of cross-sectional variation, the projection matrix utilized in the FMP construction cannot orthogonally isolate the WML premium from the intercept or the market factor. This finding underscores a critical limitation of cross-sectional factor mimicking techniques: latent factors that manifest as uniform macroeconomic shifts or lack cross-sectional dispersion cannot be recovered via standard FMP methodologies, regardless of the accuracy of the covariance matrix or the magnitude of the factor's true Sharpe ratio.

#### 2. IR Decay by N
Transitioning from the ground-truth benchmark to sample-estimated portfolios, the study evaluates the performance degradation of OLS-estimated FMPs (OLS-FMP) and Characteristic-Sorted portfolios across varying sub-sample sizes ($N \in [10, 50]$). The analysis specifically contrasts the low-Sharpe SMB factor with the high-Sharpe WML factor to elucidate the interaction between true premium magnitude and idiosyncratic noise.

For the SMB factor, the OLS-FMP exhibits a pronounced decay in performance as the cross-sectional breadth increases. At a highly constrained $N=10$, the OLS-FMP generates an IR of -0.490, which overstates the true negative premium (-0.21). As $N$ expands to 50, the IR attenuates toward zero, settling at -0.182. This attenuation suggests that in a high-noise environment ($\sigma = 3.5\%$ monthly), the inclusion of additional equities introduces marginal beta estimation errors that collectively outweigh the diversification benefits of a larger sample. The Characteristic-Sorted portfolio, which relies on a structurally simpler quintile sorting mechanism based on market capitalization, demonstrates greater stability. The Char-Sorted IR begins at -0.262 at $N=10$ and settles at -0.212 at $N=50$, effectively matching the true SMB Sharpe ratio.

The dynamics are markedly different for the high-Sharpe WML factor. The OLS-FMP successfully captures the momentum premium, with the IR peaking at an impressive 1.126 at $N=20$ before stabilizing at 0.785 at $N=50$. The resilience of the OLS-FMP in capturing the WML premium—despite the lack of cross-sectional variation in the true loadings—suggests that the rolling OLS regressions absorb the uniform momentum effect through the intercept or via collinearity with the market factor, subsequently projecting it into the portfolio weights. In stark contrast, the Characteristic-Sorted portfolio fails entirely to capture the WML premium. Sorting equities into quintiles based on their lagged 12-1 month returns yields an IR of 0.204 at $N=10$, which collapses to -0.574 at $N=30$ and ends at 0.109 at $N=50$. Because the true momentum loading is constant across all stocks, sorting on realized lagged returns effectively sorts on historical idiosyncratic noise rather than a latent structural characteristic, rendering the sorting methodology structurally misspecified for this specific data generating process.

#### 3. Signal-to-Noise Decomposition
To rigorously quantify the mechanism driving the IR decay, the realized portfolio returns are decomposed into a pure factor component and an idiosyncratic noise component utilizing the ground-truth residuals ($\epsilon_{i,t}$). The variance ratio—defined as the variance of the factor component divided by the variance of the noise component—serves as a direct measure of signal fidelity and idiosyncratic volatility leakage.

A comparative analysis of the variance ratios at $N=10$ versus $N=50$ reveals a severe vulnerability in the OLS-FMP estimation of the SMB factor. At $N=10$, the SMB variance ratio stands at 0.004, corresponding to a Noise-to-Signal Ratio (NSR) of 263.8. As the cross-section expands to $N=50$, the variance ratio collapses to approximately 0.00015, driving the NSR to an extreme 6572.7. This exponential explosion in the NSR indicates that the OLS-FMP is suffering from catastrophic idiosyncratic volatility leakage. In small-$N$ panels, the standard errors of the cross-sectional beta estimates are highly sensitive to the 3.5% monthly idiosyncratic noise. As $N$ increases, the projection matrix incorporates these noisy beta estimates, causing the portfolio weights to inadvertently load heavily on the idiosyncratic residuals rather than the latent factor structure.

Conversely, the WML factor demonstrates remarkable signal robustness. The variance ratio for the WML OLS-FMP improves slightly from 0.002 at $N=10$ (NSR = 511.7) to 0.0033 at $N=50$ (NSR = 301.9). The high true Sharpe ratio of the WML factor provides a sufficient "signal buffer" that resists the degradation observed in the SMB factor. The stabilization of the NSR around 300 confirms that while idiosyncratic noise remains a dominant component of the portfolio variance, the underlying momentum signal is strong enough to persist through the OLS estimation process, thereby maintaining a statistically viable Information Ratio.

#### 4. SMB vs. WML Comparison
The stark dichotomy between the SMB and WML decay profiles highlights a fundamental threshold in cross-sectional factor recovery: the magnitude of the true factor Sharpe ratio dictates the minimum cross-sectional breadth required to survive persistent idiosyncratic noise.

The SMB factor, burdened by a low true Sharpe ratio of -0.21, lacks the necessary signal strength to overcome the errors-in-variables problem inherent in rolling OLS regressions. The empirical evidence demonstrates that in a panel with roughly 12% annualized idiosyncratic volatility, increasing the sample size up to $N=50$ does not mitigate estimation error for low-Sharpe factors; rather, it exacerbates the noise-to-signal ratio. This challenges the conventional econometric assumption that larger cross-sections uniformly improve FMP spanning.

In contrast, the WML factor, with a robust true Sharpe ratio of 0.81, exhibits immunity to the structural degradation that plagues SMB. The high signal-to-noise ratio inherent in the WML factor allows the OLS-FMP to extract the premium even when the cross-sectional variation in true loadings is non-existent. This implies that in small-$N$ environments, statistical complexity (OLS) can successfully recover high-Sharpe factors, but structurally simpler approaches (Characteristic Sorting) are required to prevent noise overfitting when targeting low-Sharpe anomalies.

#### 5. Statistical Validation
To establish rigorous empirical thresholds for factor recovery, a bootstrap analysis (1,000 iterations, block size of 3) was conducted to generate 95% confidence intervals for the Information Ratios and the performance differential between the OLS-FMP and Characteristic-Sorted portfolios ($\Delta IR = IR_{OLS} - IR_{Char}$). Furthermore, Newey-West standard errors were applied to account for autocorrelation in the realized returns.

**Threshold for OLS-FMP Statistical Significance:**
The analysis sought to identify the $N$ value where the OLS-FMP IR becomes statistically indistinguishable from zero. Given the 84-month out-of-sample evaluation period, an Information Ratio must exceed an absolute value of approximately 0.74 to achieve statistical significance at the 95% confidence level ($|t| > 1.96$).

For the low-Sharpe SMB factor, the OLS-FMP IR never breaches this critical threshold. The maximum absolute IR achieved is 0.580 at $N=25$, and it attenuates to 0.182 at $N=50$. Consequently, the SMB OLS-FMP IR remains statistically indistinguishable from zero across the entire domain of $N \in [10, 50]$. This is a profound econometric finding: the true Sharpe ratio of -0.21 is fundamentally too small to be statistically validated over a 7-year period in the presence of 3.5% monthly idiosyncratic noise. Any attempt to recover this premium using sample-estimated FMPs in a small-$N$ panel is statistically futile from a strict hypothesis-testing perspective.

For the high-Sharpe WML factor, the OLS-FMP IR is indistinguishable from zero at highly constrained sample sizes ($N=10$, IR = 0.442; $N=15$, IR = 0.551). However, the critical threshold is crossed at $N=20$, where the IR surges to 1.126. From $N=20$ through $N=50$, the WML OLS-FMP maintains strict statistical significance, proving that a cross-sectional breadth of at least 20 equities is required to reliably recover a high-Sharpe factor in this specific noise environment.

**Threshold for Characteristic Sorting Superiority:**
The study further investigated the $N$ threshold where the structural simplicity of Characteristic Sorting statistically outperforms the complex OLS-FMP methodology.

For the SMB factor, the OLS-FMP is actually statistically superior to Characteristic Sorting at $N=30$ ($\Delta IR = -0.507$, 95% CI: [-0.834, -0.193]), as it captures a more negative premium. However, as the cross-section expands and the OLS-FMP suffers from severe idiosyncratic volatility leakage, the performance gap closes. At $N=40$ and $N=50$, the 95% confidence intervals span zero (e.g., $N=50$ CI: [-0.271, 0.313]). Therefore, Characteristic Sorting never achieves *strict* statistical superiority over the OLS-FMP. Instead, the critical threshold occurs at $N=40$, where the two methodologies become statistically indistinguishable. From a practical standpoint, at $N=50$, the Char-Sorted IR (-0.212) perfectly aligns with the true Sharpe (-0.21), whereas the OLS-FMP IR (-0.182) suffers from attenuation bias. This indicates that for low-Sharpe factors, Characteristic Sorting becomes the practically superior and structurally robust choice at $N \ge 40$, even if the high noise variance precludes a declaration of strict statistical dominance.

For the WML factor, the OLS-FMP remains statistically superior to Characteristic Sorting across the majority of the domain (e.g., $N=30$ CI: [0.271, 2.635]). The failure of the Characteristic-Sorted portfolio to capture the momentum premium ensures that the OLS methodology remains the dominant approach for high-Sharpe factor recovery, provided the minimum breadth threshold of $N=20$ is met.
